In March, the Town of Rome in Adams County did everything civic life tells a community to do. Residents gathered more than 2,000 signatures against a 200-megawatt solar project and filed more than 200 public comments. On March 5, 2026, the Public Service Commission approved the project unanimously.

The effort was real. It was aimed at the wrong address. In Wisconsin, a utility-scale energy project is decided by the PSC, not the town, so two thousand signatures landed on a desk with no authority over the outcome.

Rome isn't an outlier. In the Town of Morgan, in Oconto County, two town board members were recalled in 2022 in a fight over a solar project, and 67 percent of voters opposed it in an April 2023 referendum. The same developer, NextEra Energy Resources, received its PSC approval anyway, with a final decision on July 6, 2026.

Much of my own work is on data centers, and it would be easy to read the past year in Wisconsin as a data center story. I'd read it differently. Organized opposition has become the expected response to contested development of almost every kind: solar, wind, housing, data centers. The subject changes from project to project. The pattern doesn't, and three parts of it keep showing up.

The first is where the energy goes. Before a community asks what it thinks of a project, it has to ask where the decision actually gets made and what its standing is in that room. A town that knows it will be an intervenor at the PSC prepares an intervenor's case: infrastructure data, siting conditions, the terms it wants in an agreement. A town that thinks it's the decision-maker prepares a petition. Both of the communities referenced above worked hard. Only one kind of work counts in the room where the vote happens.

Marathon County faces the same limit with Alliant Energy's proposed Hub City Wind project, about 150 megawatts across six towns in the southwestern part of the county, which will also go to the PSC. Instead of a petition, the county board set up a local impact advisory committee, held a public forum on June 17, 2026, and ran a resident survey through June 19. The stated purpose was to guide the county in negotiating the terms of an agreement with the developer. On October 1, the board voted 24 to 7 to ask Alliant for a proposed contract that answers the committee's concerns, with a November 1 deadline. Whatever comes of it, the county is negotiating from a written record. Marathon County has exactly as much, or as little, authority over a PSC project as the Town of Rome had. The actions taken in response, however, illustrate a very different posture.

The second part of the pattern is that the objection is often about process before it's about the project. Invenergy began approaching farmers about land leases for the Koshkonong Solar Energy Center, in the Towns of Christiana and Deerfield in Dane County, in late 2018. The application reached the PSC on April 15, 2021, and was approved on May 5, 2022. One resident described the project as moving forward "under the cover of darkness." Once people believe a deal was put together without them, the first question in the room becomes why they're only hearing about it now, and whether the project is any good has to wait behind it. Data center proposals have run the same course, which is much of what I wrote about in [Lessons from DeForest](https://nascconsulting.com/insights/lessons-from-deforest/).

The third is that the cost outlives the project. In 2023, the City of Altoona annexed land from the neighboring Town of Washington for Whitetail Woods, a subdivision mixing single-family homes, twinhomes and multifamily units. At the public hearing, residents raised traffic, density and strain on emergency services. The city council approved the preliminary plat 5 to 0 in July 2024. By then the town had begun working to incorporate as a village, to protect what land it had left from annexation by Altoona and Eau Claire. In April 2026, the state's incorporation review board dismissed the petition. Somewhere along the way, the argument stopped being about a subdivision and became one about where the municipal line sits. Everyone involved paid for that fight.

Every one of these communities worked hard. What most of them didn't have was a set of answers settled before anyone needed them.

I closed the Wisconsin Economic Development Association's Demystifying Development webinar series in October with a line I'll repeat here: readiness isn't a document you finish. It's a short list of decisions you make now, or make badly under pressure later.

In August I wrote about [five decisions a board can make before anyone calls](https://nascconsulting.com/insights/five-decisions-before-anyone-calls/), all about how a community handles information and its own voice. They still hold. The list below folds most of them into three items and adds two that the cases above kept pointing to: what your systems can serve and what your code allows. Neither one is a study. Each is a decision about what you already know and what you've already written down.

1. **What can we actually support?** Water, wastewater, power and roads, at whatever scale you expect. For a subdivision, that might be whether the sewer takes another 200 homes and whether emergency services can reach them, which is exactly what Altoona's residents asked.
2. **Are the project types we expect defined as land uses?** If your ordinance has no definition and no permit path for what's coming, the silence doesn't read as neutral. It reads as unprepared.
3. **What is our information and notification policy?** What confidentiality terms you will and won't sign, and how many days after a formal inquiry the public hears about it from you. A community can't stop private land assembly. It can decide how fast residents hear from the people they elected.
4. **Who is on the bench?** A planning consultant, land use counsel, and fiscal and environmental analysts, identified before you need them and already familiar with your comprehensive plan.
5. **Does everyone agree on how this runs?** The elected body, staff, the clerk, planning and counsel, agreed in advance on how inquiries are handled, who speaks and when, and who does what once a private conversation becomes a public process. This is the one that protects the staff in the middle, because it turns freelancing into following a process everyone signed.

None of the five needs a bigger staff, and every one can be decided in a room with no money on the table.

Wausau shows what that looks like. With no data center proposal in front of it, the city wrote its rules first. The Plan Commission endorsed them on June 16, 2026, and the City Council adopted them 11 to 0 on July 14. Data centers are limited to medium and heavy industrial districts, each one needs a conditional use permit, and a 200-foot buffer separates them from residential property. An applicant has to bring five studies: water use and heat management, wastewater, sound, a sustainability plan, and traffic. Assistant City Planner Carrie Edmondson put the whole idea in two sentences: "We're not saying they are allowed or they're not allowed. We're saying you need to prove it to us."

Wausau didn't decide whether it wants a data center. It decided what any applicant has to show before that question comes up, and the same move works for a subdivision, a quarry or a wind farm.

That matters on both sides of the table. A developer walking into a community that has made these decisions knows who it's talking to, what the community needs and what the path looks like. That's a tougher negotiation in some ways, but it's one both sides can finish, and the terms tend to hold. Prepared and hostile are different things. A prepared community is legible, and it can say yes or no on its own terms and explain either answer to its residents.

If you already have a proposal on your desk, the list still applies. Making these decisions while a project is live is harder and more public, but it works, and it beats making them one board meeting at a time.

Start with the one your community could realistically settle in the next 90 days. The other four get easier once one exists.
